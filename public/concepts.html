01

The problem RAG solves

A language model knows what it was trained on. It does not know your documents, your chat history, or anything written after its training finished.

The obvious fix is to paste everything into the question. That fails quickly. Models have a limit on how much text they can read at once, long inputs cost more and answer more slowly, and burying one relevant sentence in a hundred pages makes the answer worse rather than better.

Retrieval augmented generation is the alternative. Rather than showing the model everything, you find the handful of passages that actually relate to the question and show it only those. The name describes the order of operations: retrieve first, then use what you retrieved to augment the prompt, then generate an answer.

Everything that follows is about the retrieval half, because that is the half that decides whether the answer is any good. A model given the right three paragraphs will do well. The same model given three irrelevant paragraphs will produce something confident and wrong.

02

The pipeline in one picture

Four things happen, twice over. Your documents go through the top row once, ahead of time. Your question goes through the bottom row every time you ask something.

Chunkie walks you through exactly these four steps, in this order, using a chat transcript as the document. The rest of this guide explains each one properly.

03

Chunking

Why cut anything up

Search returns whole pieces. If your entire archive were one piece, every question would match it equally well and you would learn nothing about where the answer actually is. Cutting the document into smaller pieces is what lets a search point at a specific passage.

A chunk is one of those pieces. It is the unit of everything that follows: one chunk becomes one vector, gets one score, and is retrieved or not retrieved as a whole.

The trade-off

Small chunks are precise but lose context. A chunk containing only "yes, do that" has lost the question it was answering, so it matches nothing useful. Large chunks keep context but dilute it: one relevant sentence among twenty gets averaged away by the other nineteen. Choosing chunk size is one of the few decisions that genuinely changes how well a RAG system works.

The three strategies

StrategyHow it cutsBest when
Per message One chunk per message. Messages are self contained. Precise, but short replies lose their question.
Fixed window The document becomes one long string, sliced every N characters with an overlap. The general case, and what most production systems use. Ignores structure entirely.
Grouped N consecutive messages per chunk. Question and answer need to stay together. Uses the structure the document already has.

Overlap

Fixed windows have a problem: a sentence that straddles a boundary gets split in half, and neither half retrieves well. Overlap is the fix. Each chunk repeats the last few hundred characters of the one before it, so any given sentence appears whole in at least one chunk. 

// size 200, overlap 60
chunk 1  [ 0 .................... 200 ]
chunk 2            [ 140 .................... 340 ]
chunk 3                      [ 280 .................... 480 ]
                    ^^^^^^
         these 60 characters appear in both chunks

The cost is storage and duplication: with 30 percent overlap you are embedding roughly 30 percent more text. That is usually worth it. In Chunkie, overlapping text is tinted blue in the chunk list so you can see the repetition rather than take it on trust.

04

Embeddings

This is the idea everything else rests on, and it is simpler than it sounds.

Tokens first

Models do not read characters or words. They read tokens: common chunks of text, roughly three quarters of a word each on average. "Delivery" might be one token, "reimbursed" might be two. This matters mainly because model limits are counted in tokens, and because it explains why models are sometimes strange about spelling.

What a vector is

An embedding is a list of numbers that stands for a piece of text. That list is called a vector, and how many numbers it holds is its dimensions. The model Chunkie uses produces 384 of them per chunk.

A vector is a direction. With two numbers you can point somewhere on a page. With three you can point somewhere in a room. With 384 you can point somewhere in a space nobody can picture, but the arithmetic works exactly the same way.

The useful property is this: text with similar meaning gets a similar list, even when the words are completely different. That is not a coincidence. It is what the model was trained to do, over a very large amount of text.

A worked example. These are real cosine scores from the model Chunkie ships with:

"the cat sat on the mat" against "a feline rested on a rug"0.56
"the cat sat on the mat" against "quarterly revenue rose 12 percent"0.02

The first pair shares almost no words. The second pair shares "the". Word counting gets this exactly backwards; the embedding gets it right.

Where meaning lives

A reasonable question: does dimension 47 mean "politeness"? Almost never. Individual dimensions are not interpretable, and nobody assigned them meanings. Meaning is distributed across the whole pattern, which is why Chunkie draws a vector as a coloured strip rather than asking you to read 384 numbers. You are meant to compare the shape of two strips, not read any single cell.

05

Measuring similarity

Cosine similarity

Once both your question and every chunk are vectors, comparing them is geometry. The usual measure is cosine similarity: the cosine of the angle between two vectors. It asks whether two arrows point the same way, and deliberately ignores how long they are.

The formula is a dot product divided by the two lengths. When vectors are already scaled to length one, which most embedding models do for you, it collapses to just the dot product: multiply the pairs and add them up. 

function cosineSimilarity(a, b) {
  let dot = 0, normA = 0, normB = 0;
  for (let i = 0; i < a.length; i++) {
    dot   += a[i] * b[i];
    normA += a[i] * a[i];
    normB += b[i] * b[i];
  }
  return dot / (Math.sqrt(normA) * Math.sqrt(normB));
}

That is the entire search engine. There is no index and no clever data structure: score every chunk, sort, take the top few. On a few hundred chunks it finishes in under a millisecond, which is why Chunkie can afford to re-run it on every character you type.

Reading the scores

Scores are relative, not absolute. There is no universal threshold that means "relevant", and the useful signal is usually the gap between the top result and the rest. A clear winner means the document really does answer you. A flat spread of mediocre scores usually means it does not.

RoughlyUsually means
0.7 and aboveVery close, often near paraphrase
0.4 to 0.7Related. Most genuinely useful hits sit here
0.2 to 0.4Loosely connected, same general subject
below 0.2Little in common

Treat those bands as orientation rather than law. They shift with the model, the language and the length of your chunks, which is exactly why you should look at your own numbers instead of trusting a table.

06

Drawing 384 dimensions

Nobody can picture 384 dimensions, so the map in Chunkie flattens them to two. The method is principal component analysis, and the idea is less intimidating than the name.

Imagine a cloud of points in 3D and a torch. Shine it from different angles and you get different shadows on the wall. Most angles squash the cloud into a blob. One angle spreads it out the most. PCA finds that angle, then the best angle at right angles to it, and uses those two as the x and y axes.

So the axes have no names. They are simply the two directions along which your particular chunks differ most. Distance on the map is real: dots near each other really are close in meaning. But two dimensions cannot hold everything 384 did, so the map is an honest summary rather than the whole truth. Two dots that look close may be further apart than they appear.

Worth knowing

The search itself never uses the map. Scoring happens in all 384 dimensions. The projection exists purely so that you can see the structure, and it is computed after the fact.

07

Retrieval and the prompt

Retrieval is the part with the fewest surprises. Score every chunk, sort by score, keep the best few. That count is usually called top k, and k is typically somewhere between three and ten. Chunkie uses five.

In a production system the vectors live in a vector database, which uses an approximate nearest neighbour index so it does not have to score millions of rows one by one. It trades a little accuracy for a great deal of speed. Chunkie has no database because with a few hundred chunks an exhaustive scan is already instant, and hiding the comparison inside a library would defeat the purpose of the project.

The retrieved chunks then get assembled into a prompt. This is the augmented part of the name, and it is usually just string concatenation: 

Answer the question using only the context below.

CONTEXT:
[1] ...highest scoring chunk...
[2] ...second...
[3] ...third...

QUESTION: what the person actually asked

The instruction to use only the context is what keeps the model anchored to your documents rather than to its own recollection. Chunkie shows you this assembled prompt at the end of the search step, but deliberately stops there and never calls a model. The retrieval half is the half worth understanding, and it is the half people skip.

08

Where RAG falls down

A guide that only lists strengths is not much use. These are the failure modes you will meet in practice, most of which you can reproduce in Chunkie in under a minute.

There is always a nearest chunk

Ask about something the document never mentions and you still get results, because something always scores highest. Look at the scores, not the count. This is why real systems set a minimum score and say they do not know.

Bad chunking cannot be recovered

If a fact is split across two chunks, no amount of clever searching reassembles it. Retrieval quality is capped by the decision you made before any of it ran.

Meaning search is bad at exact strings

Order numbers, error codes and rare names are precisely what embeddings smooth away. Serious systems pair vector search with keyword search for this reason.

Questions and answers look different

A question often resembles other questions more than it resembles its own answer, so a question in your document can outrank the reply that answers it. Grouped chunks help.

Retrieval does not fix hallucination

Giving a model the right passage makes a wrong answer less likely. It does not make one impossible, and a confident answer built on an irrelevant passage is the worst outcome of all.

Embeddings inherit their training

A model trained mostly on English handles other languages less well, and a general model handles specialist vocabulary less well. Test on your own material.

09

Inside Chunkie

Everything above is general. This part is specific: how this project is built, and why.

The architecture

There is no backend. The embedding model runs inside your browser tab through transformers.js, which executes an ONNX model on WebAssembly. The vectors live in a JavaScript array. Nothing is uploaded anywhere, and there is no server, no database and no account. 

Your browser tab
  main thread                          web worker
  ───────────                          ──────────
  parse the file
  cut into chunks
  send chunk texts        ──────────▶  load model (once, then cached)
                                       embed a batch
                          ◀──────────  send the batch back
                                       ...repeat...
  score every chunk
  project to 2D
  draw

The model runs in a web worker because it is genuinely slow work. On the main thread it would freeze the page while it ran. Vectors are streamed back one batch at a time rather than held until the end, which is why you can watch them appear.

Where each idea lives

Every concept in this guide has one file that implements it, and nothing else touches it.

IdeaFileWhat is in it

Chunkingsrc/lib/chunk.tsThe three strategies, word boundary snapping, and overlap accounting
Cosine and PCAsrc/lib/vector.tsSimilarity scoring, plus a power iteration projection to two dimensions

The pipelinesrc/hooks/useRagPipeline.tsAll the state, and the guards that stop stale results landing

Decisions worth knowing

The model is quantised
all-MiniLM-L6-v2 at 8 bit precision, roughly 23 MB. Full precision would be about four times larger for no visible difference at this scale. It downloads once and your browser caches it.
Search runs on every keystroke
Embedding one short question takes about a millisecond and scoring several hundred chunks takes under one, so there is nothing worth debouncing and no reason for a search button. Requests carry a sequence number so a slower one cannot overwrite a newer one.
PCA is written out rather than imported
Around eighty lines of power iteration in vector.ts. A library would have been fewer lines and less readable, and readability is the point of the project.
Cross origin isolation headers are set locally too
vite.config.ts sets the same COOP and COEP headers in dev and preview. They let the WASM runtime use its multi threaded backend. Setting them in only one environment is a reliable way to ship something that works on your machine and nowhere else.
10

Glossary

Chunk
One retrievable piece of a document. The unit that gets embedded, scored and returned.
Context window
The maximum amount of text a model can read in one go, counted in tokens.
Cosine similarity
How closely two vectors point the same way. Runs from 1 through 0 to -1, and ignores length.
Dimensions
How many numbers are in a vector. 384 for the model here.
Embedding
A vector that stands for a piece of text, arranged so similar meanings sit near each other.
Hallucination
A confident answer that is not supported by the source. Retrieval reduces it and does not remove it.
Overlap
Text repeated between neighbouring chunks so sentences are not cut in half at a boundary.
PCA
Principal component analysis. Finds the directions along which data varies most, used here to draw 384 dimensions as 2.
RAG
Retrieval augmented generation. Retrieve relevant passages, add them to the prompt, then generate.
Semantic search
Searching by meaning rather than by matching words.
Token
The unit a model reads. Roughly three quarters of a word on average.
Top k
How many of the highest scoring chunks to keep. Five in Chunkie.
Vector database
Storage built for finding nearest vectors quickly across millions of rows, using an approximate index.
Web worker
A background thread in the browser. Keeps slow work from freezing the page.
